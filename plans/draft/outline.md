# Outline of the rewritten declarations design

This is the detailed outline of the rewrite of [`declarations-and-addressing-io.md`](../declarations-and-addressing-io.md), whose text at commit `8af5bd11` is the source.
The design is held fixed: every rule of the source keeps its meaning, and an ambiguity the rewrite exposes goes to Appendix A instead of being settled.
[`sample.md`](sample.md) shows the front matter and three sections in final form.

Figures named here that `make_figures.py` does not yet draw (`held-field`, `select-one`, `law`, `law-of-laws`, `function`, `batch`) are added when their sections are written.

Each section below lists, in order:
- **Defines:** the definitions it gives, each in a definition box;
- **States:** the consequences and properties it states, each in its own box;
- **Figures:** the diagrams it needs, generated by [`../figures/make_figures.py`](../figures/make_figures.py);
- **Examples:** the small examples it builds, each standalone;
- **Notes:** the collapsible technical notes it holds;
- **Source:** the sections of the source at `8af5bd11` its content comes from.

"Source § n" means a section of the source; "II.2" and the like mean sections of `design/`.

## Front matter

- **Title:** "Inputs, outputs, paths, and merge".
- **Summary**, non-technical: what the document is for (how ProbPipe's terms name their parts, receive values, offer what they produce, and combine by name), who it is for, and what each part holds.
- **Contents:** Part I, Part II, and the appendices, linked.
- **Source:** source § 1.1, rewritten without the grammar label, the Rocq lemma names, or the scope list; the status block and the scope go to Appendix F.

## Part I: The conceptual model

Part I uses no code objects.
It introduces notation only where it defines it: records as `{a: 1.0, g: {c: 2.0}}`, `t[p]` and `t[[p, q]]` for selecting, and `a & b` for merging.
Examples are written in that notation, with laws named by their family, as `mu ~ Normal(0, 5)`.

### 1. Values and the places inside them

#### 1.1 Base values and records

- **Defines:** *base value* (an array or an opaque value: a value ProbPipe names no part of); *record* (a tree of named values); *name* (a non-empty string without `/`).
- **States:** none.
- **Figures:** none.
- **Examples:** the array `2.5`; the string `"forest"`; the record `{a: 1.0, g: {c: 2.0, e: 3.0}}`.
- **Notes:** none.
- **Source:** source § 2.1 (rows record, opaque value), § 2.3; III.1, III.2, III.5.

#### 1.2 Paths, nodes, fields, and groups

- **Defines:** *node* and *root*; *path* (the `/`-joined names from the root down to a node; the root has none); *child* and *canonical order* (II.6's meanings); *field* (a node of a record that is not itself a record); *group* (a node, other than the root, that is a record); *leaf path* (a plain phrase, defined in passing).
- **States:** every node has one path, since sibling names are distinct.
- **Figures:** `record-tree` (the record `{a, g: {c, e}}` drawn as a tree, with its paths, fields, and group listed).
- **Examples:** the paths, fields, and group of `{a: 1.0, g: {c: 2.0, e: 3.0}}`.
- **Notes:** none.
- **Source:** source § 2.1 (the words *child*, *canonical order*, *name*), § 2.4 (first table).

#### 1.3 The parts of a value

- **Defines:** *part* (a node below the root; the parts of a value are its nodes at its paths).
- **States:** a base value has no parts, so it has no paths.
- **Figures:** none.
- **Examples:** the record above has four parts; the array `2.5` has none.
- **Notes:** "Why ProbPipe names no part of an array" (an array's entries are indexed by position, which is not a path).
- **Source:** source § 2.3 (the word "parts"), § 4.1 (a base value has no paths). See Appendix A, question A3.

### 2. Terms, kinds, and types

#### 2.1 Terms and their kinds

- **Defines:** *term* (anything ProbPipe tracks); *kind*, with the five kinds: *value* (a base value or a record), *law* (a probability distribution, which takes no inputs), *kernel* (a law for each value of what it conditions on), *function* (a tracked callable), and *batch* (many terms of one kind, stacked along named axes called *levels*; one of them is an *element*); *label* (a display name that no operation reads).
- **States:** none.
- **Figures:** none.
- **Examples:** one line each: `{a: 1.0}`; `Normal(0, 1)`; `lik(y | mu)`, which draws `y ~ Normal(mu, 1)`; a function from `theta` to `{mean, var}`; 100 draws of a law.
- **Notes:** none.
- **Source:** source § 2.1 (rows term, kind, law, event, draw, batch, kernel, function, label).

#### 2.2 What a term produces

- **Defines:** *produced term*, stated once for all kinds: a value produces itself; a law a random draw; a kernel a random draw at each value of its inputs; a function its return value at each value of its inputs; a batch itself. *Draw* and *event* (what one draw is) are defined here.
- **States:** a statement about a law's produced term is a statement about the law of a draw; a law's produced term is a draw, not the law, which is the reading a joint law needs.
- **Figures:** none.
- **Examples:** a draw of a law over `{mu, tau}` is a record `{mu, tau}`; the return value of a function from `theta` to `{mean, var}` at one `theta`.
- **Notes:** none.
- **Source:** source § 2.5 (first table and its paragraph).

#### 2.3 The type of a term

- **Defines:** *type* (*term spec*): what every term of a kind with that type shares. The *base types*: an array type (a shape, and optionally a dtype and a support) and an opaque type. A *record type* (the record's tree with a type at each field). A law's type states its *event type*, the type of one draw. A kernel's type states its inputs' types and its event type. A function's type states its inputs' types and its return type, either of which may be unknown. A batch's type states its element's type and its levels. *Dimension name* (an array size given by a name, such as `n_obs`) and *dimension scope* (within one scope, a name stands for one size).
- **States:** every law has an event type, whether or not it can be sampled.
- **Figures:** none.
- **Examples:** the type of `{a: 1.0, g: {c: 2.0, e: 3.0}}`; the event type of a law over `{mu, tau}`; the type `("n_obs", "features")` and its one dimension scope with `("features",)`.
- **Notes:** "Dimension scopes in full" (II.1's rule: the first occurrence binds a name, a later one that disagrees is refused, two different free names at one axis are refused).
- **Source:** source § 2.1 (rows term spec, symbolic dimension), § 2.3, § 2.8 (what each type holds, without `held`), § 2.14 (dimension scope paragraph); II.1, III.5, III.7.

#### 2.4 The paths of every term

- **Defines:** *the paths of a type*: a record type's paths are its tree's; a law's and a kernel's are those of its event type; a function's are those of its return type; a batch's are those of its element's type. *The paths of a term* are the paths of its type. *Column* (the node of a batch at a path: the batch, over the same levels, of each element's node there).
- **States:** a path continues through a field that holds a law, a kernel, a function, or a batch, into that term's own paths; a record's fields are still only its own, so the record `{n: 3.0, G: post}` has the fields `n` and `G` and the paths `n`, `G`, `G/mu`, `G/tau`. A function whose return type is unknown has no paths known before it is called.
- **Figures:** `held-field` (a record whose field `G` holds a law over `{mu, tau}`, with the path `G/mu` continuing into the law).
- **Examples:** the paths of a law over `{a, g: {c, e}}`; the record `{n: 3.0, G: post}`; the column `mu` of 100 draws of a law over `{mu, tau}`.
- **Notes:** "The paths of a type, stated by cases" (the precise statement of source § 2.10 as a case list, with the law over a single array having no path below its root).
- **Source:** source § 2.4 ("Paths go through every kind"), § 2.5 (columns), § 2.10 (rewritten without `spec_at`; the law's paths defined from its event type, not from its draws).

#### 2.5 When one type admits another: conformance and unification

- **Defines:** *conforms* (a directional relation: every value of type `A` is admitted by type `B`, in one dimension scope); *unify* (a symmetric relation whose result, the *unification*, admits exactly the values both admit).
- **States:** a type conforms to the unification of `A` and `B` exactly when it conforms to both (with the stated proviso on free dimension names); unification is symmetric, and associative where defined; a type declared by sizes alone sets no dtype or support, so it conforms to and unifies with any type of that shape.
- **Figures:** none.
- **Examples:** `float32` with `float64` unifies to `float32`; a `(3,)` array conforms to `("p",)`, binding `p` to 3; `(4,)` does not conform to `(3,)`.
- **Notes:** "Conformance by kind" (the seven cases); "Unification by kind" (the seven cases); "Dtypes and supports" (NumPy's same-kind and safe-cast rules, supports not compared).
- **Source:** source § 2.14 (conformance and unification).

### 3. Inputs and outputs

Shown in final form in [`sample.md`](sample.md).

#### 3.1 Inputs: where a term receives values

- **Defines:** *input* (a named place where a term receives one value: a name that is a Python identifier, a type, and a flag, required or optional); *optional input* (an input with a default, which the term that owns the input holds); *binding* (supplying values to some of a term's inputs, by name).
- **States:** which kinds have inputs: kernels and functions, and batches of them; values and laws have none. Binding a kernel's last required input gives a law. An input whose type is a record type is one input.
- **Figures:** none.
- **Examples:** the kernel `lik_opt(y | mu, s)` with `s` optional and default 1; binding `mu = 0.3`.
- **Notes:** "A record input, and binding part of it" (paths into a record input; binding part of one makes the input required).
- **Source:** source § 2.6; III.9, IV.4.

#### 3.2 Outputs: what a term offers

- **Defines:** *output* (a name under which a term offers part or all of what it produces); *the value at an output*.
- **States:** which parts a term offers depends only on what it produces; the rule follows in § 3.3.
- **Figures:** none.
- **Examples:** the law over `{mu, tau}` offers `mu` and `tau`.
- **Notes:** none.
- **Source:** source § 2.11 (value at an output), § 2.9 (first rows).

#### 3.3 How outputs are named

- **Defines:** *the naming rule* (what each kind of produced term offers: a base value one output at the root, under a name its producer states; a record its children; a function the outputs of its return value; a batch the outputs of its elements; a law or a kernel none; nothing but a base value is named from outside); *output at the root*; *wrap* (producing the one-field record `{name: value}` to offer a value under one name).
- **States:** a term offers one output at the root or outputs that are all one-name paths, never both; the name of an output at the root is not a path; every other output's name is its own path, so a term has one set of paths; a base value that no term produced has no outputs.
- **Figures:** `output-naming` (a kernel offering its array draw `y` at the root; a law offering `beta` and `sigma`; the same law wrapped under `params`).
- **Examples:** the three of the figure; a function into records `{mean, var}`; a function that returns a law; 100 draws of a law over `{mu, tau}`; 4 draws of a law over one array named `x`.
- **Notes:** "Why a function into records offers its outputs and a law over records does not" (a function and a batch are determined by their outputs; a joint law is not determined by its marginals); "Output names and input names" (any name versus identifiers).
- **Source:** source § 1.1 (rule 3), § 2.7 (the rule and its reason), § 2.11 (outputs, the principle, why function versus law, the output at the root, the guarantee), § 2.9 (the table, as a consequence), § 6.20 Q2(ii).

#### 3.4 The input spec and the output spec

- **Defines:** *input spec* (a term's inputs, in order, with their types and optional flags; no defaults); *output spec*, and *event spec* for a law's or a kernel's (the type of a term's produced term, and the name of its output at the root when that type is a base type); *type of an output* (the type of the value at that output); *type hole* (an output named before its type is known); *record of outputs* (a produced term read as the record of its outputs: a record is itself, a base value `v` under `n` is `{n: v}`).
- **States:** the type alone decides whether an output spec names an output at the root; input specs are equal regardless of order, output specs are not, since the order of outputs is the order of the record's fields; the type of each output, read from the output spec, by kind of produced term; the record of outputs gives back the produced term.
- **Figures:** none.
- **Examples:** the output spec of the kernel `lik(y | mu)` and of the law over `{mu, tau}`; the input spec of `lik_opt`.
- **Notes:** "Equality of input specs and output specs" (source § 4.14).
- **Source:** source § 2.6 (input spec), § 2.7 (output spec, type holes), § 2.11 (the spec of an output), § 2.12 (`rec` and `dec`, without the notation), § 4.14.

#### 3.5 A term's signature, and how a figure draws it

- **Defines:** *signature* (a term's outputs and inputs, written `name(outputs | inputs)`).
- **States:** none.
- **Figures:** `legend-terms` (the conventions for terms: direction of flow, box and badge, output ports, the output at the root, input ports, hollow optional ports, the parts tree, the batch stack); `signature` (`lik_opt(y | mu, s)`).
- **Examples:** the signature of `lik_opt`.
- **Notes:** none.
- **Source:** the brief's visual language; source § 2.1 (row "label": notation such as `lik(y | mu)`).

#### 3.6 Inputs and outputs compared

- **Defines:** none.
- **States:** the comparison table (one value serves many receivers, one value needs one source; identifiers versus any names; every input named, only a base value named from outside; flat inputs, outputs with paths below; optional inputs, outputs always present; no type holes in inputs; order-free versus ordered equality; a term with no inputs is valid, an output spec never describes an empty record).
- **Figures:** none.
- **Examples:** none.
- **Notes:** none.
- **Source:** source § 2.23 (the rows that do not depend on merge or on code names; rows 3, 4, 15, 16 move to § 5, rows 9, 18, 19, 20 to Part II).

### 4. Selecting, removing, and renaming parts

#### 4.1 Selecting one part

- **Defines:** *selection at a path*, `t[p]`: for a value, the node at `p` itself; for a law, a kernel, a function, or a batch, the term of the same kind whose produced term is the node at `p` of `t`'s produced term. *Marginal law* (a law's selection at `p`).
- **States:** selecting commutes with producing (for a law, the selection's draws are distributed as the draws' nodes, at every path, through held fields too); paths chain, `t[p][q]` is `t[p/q]`; selecting keeps a term's inputs; positions and paths commute on a batch.
- **Figures:** `select-one` (a law over `{a, g: {c, e}}` and its selection at `g`, a law offering `c` and `e`).
- **Examples:** a law over `{a, g: {c, e}}` at `g` and at `g/c`; the function from `theta` to `{mean, var}` at `mean`; the record `{n: 3.0, G: post}` at `G/mu`.
- **Notes:** none.
- **Source:** source § 4.2, § 2.4 (held fields); invariants I2, I4, I5, I13, I14.

#### 4.2 Selecting several parts at once

- **Defines:** *selection at a list of paths*, `t[[p, q]]`: the record, or the term whose produced term is the record, of the chosen nodes, each at its own path, in the term's order; a path inside another is refused.
- **States:** one path gives the node, a list of one path gives a record that holds it; two parameters of the same name in two groups are both kept.
- **Figures:** none.
- **Examples:** `t[["a", "g/c"]]` over `{a, g: {c, e}}`; `t["g/c"]` against `t[["g/c"]]`; a hierarchical law at `s1/theta` and `s2/theta`.
- **Notes:** "A list that passes through a held term" (it cuts the held term down, in its own kind); "The output at the root in a list" (the extra rule: a list may name the output at the root, at the top level, and gives `{n: ...}`).
- **Source:** source § 4.3, § 4.2 (list rule), § 4.4 rule 3; invariant I9; decision O6.

#### 4.3 Removing parts

- **Defines:** *removal*, `t` without `ps`: `t` in its own kind with the nodes at `ps` removed, each other node at its path, inputs unchanged; a group or a held field all of whose leaf paths are removed goes too; removing everything is refused.
- **States:** a list of paths and removal are complements: every leaf path is in exactly one of `t[ps]` and `t` without `ps`.
- **Figures:** none.
- **Examples:** `{a, g: {c, e}}` without `g/e`; the same without `g/c` and `g/e`.
- **Notes:** none.
- **Source:** source § 4.5; invariant I10.

#### 4.4 Selecting from a type

- **Defines:** *selection from an output spec*: the output spec of `t[s]`, read from `t`'s output spec alone. One path to a base node gives the node under its last name; to a node with parts gives that node's type, offering its parts; below a held term gives a type of the held term's kind.
- **States:** the output spec of `t[s]` is the output spec of `t` at `s`; selection never adds a name the output spec does not hold; dimension names are kept as they are, and substituting a size commutes with selecting.
- **Figures:** none.
- **Examples:** the output spec of a law over `{a, g: {c, e}}` at `g/c`, at `g`, and at `[g]`.
- **Notes:** "Selecting from a type, stated by cases" (source § 2.10 case 2 and § 4.4 rules 1 to 4, with `restrict` and `remove` written as case lists, and no `held` or `with_held`); "Dimension names under selection" (source § 4.10).
- **Source:** source § 2.10, § 4.4, § 4.5 (on output specs), § 4.10; invariant I6.

#### 4.5 Renaming and moving parts

- **Defines:** *rename* (gives the node at a path a new path, which can move it; the keys are the term's paths and the name of the output at the root).
- **States:** no key can be read both as a name and as a path; the name of an output at the root cannot become a nested path.
- **Figures:** none.
- **Examples:** renaming `g/c` to `g/k`; moving `g/c` to the top level; renaming the output at the root `x` to `y`.
- **Notes:** "How renames apply" (simultaneous renames, collisions, and order, by II.6's rules, cited).
- **Source:** source § 4.7, § 4.8; II.6.

#### 4.6 The term at an output

- **Defines:** *the term at an output* `c`: `t[c]` for an output at a path, and `t` itself for the output at the root.
- **States:** the type of an output is the type of the produced term of the term at that output (for a value or a batch, of the term itself); a term whose produced term is a law offers nothing to read.
- **Figures:** none.
- **Examples:** a law over one array named `x`, whose term at `x` is the law; the term at `g` of a law over `{a, g: {c, e}}`.
- **Notes:** none.
- **Source:** source § 4.11; invariant I12; decision Q5.

### 5. Merging terms by name

Shown in final form in [`sample.md`](sample.md).

#### 5.1 Why merge

- **Defines:** none.
- **States:** the motivation: a joint model is a product of densities connected by names.
- **Figures:** none.
- **Examples:** `lik(y | mu)` and a prior over `{mu, tau}` give the law over `{y, mu, tau}`.
- **Notes:** none.
- **Source:** source § 2.15 (first paragraphs, without `*`).

#### 5.2 The definition of merge

- **Defines:** *family* (the pairs that merge: two records, two laws or kernels, two functions, two batches over equal levels; and two types of one sort); *feeds*; *shared input*; *merge* (outputs, inputs, kind of the result); *valid merge* (the eight conditions).
- **States:** the kind of the result follows from the inputs that remain.
- **Figures:** `legend-merge` (wires, fan-out, dashed boundary); `merge-general` (`a & b` with a fed name, a shared input, and remaining inputs on both sides); `merge-kernel-law` (`lik & prior`); `merge-shared-input` (`lik_nee & lik_lai`).
- **Examples:** `lik & prior`; `lik_nee & lik_lai`; `prior & lik`, refused by the order rule.
- **Notes:** "Merging types" (output specs, input specs, record types); "Factors, and the inputs a joint law's factors still take" (definition of *factor*, and of *the remaining inputs of the factors*); "Terms of two families do not merge".
- **Source:** source § 2.13, § 2.15, § 2.16, § 2.17, § 2.18, § 2.19 (R1 to R3).

#### 5.3 The value of a merge

- **Defines:** *the value of a merge*, by family: laws and kernels by sampling the right operand first, and by the product of densities; records side by side; functions as kernels whose laws are point masses; batches element by element.
- **States:** a merge of records is shallow, by top-level name.
- **Figures:** `merge-records` (two records side by side).
- **Examples:** `lik & prior` as `p(y | mu) · p(mu, tau)`; two records; two functions `center & spread`, and `standardize & (center & spread)`.
- **Notes:** "A shared optional input that a binding omits"; "A merge that leaves only optional inputs"; "Batches merge element by element"; "A deep merge of records, written by hand".
- **Source:** source § 2.20, § 2.19 (R3 to R6 and its notes), § 2.16 (batches), § 5.7 (functions), § 5.8 (batches).

#### 5.4 Why each condition holds

- **Defines:** none.
- **States:** one reason per condition.
- **Figures:** none.
- **Examples:** none beyond those of § 5.2.
- **Notes:** "Why produced terms must be values a record can hold"; "Why outputs feed only from right to left"; "A merge never lifts"; "Two selections of one law merge as independent factors".
- **Source:** source § 2.21.

#### 5.5 Properties of merge

- **Defines:** none.
- **States:** sides stay disjoint; paths are kept, and an output at the root moves to the path of its name; associative when both groupings are valid; commutative up to the order of outputs when neither operand feeds the other; sampling commutes with merge; renaming commutes with merge; labels are never read; the output spec and input spec of a merge are the merges of the operands' (after dimension binding).
- **Figures:** none.
- **Examples:** a law over one array `x` merged with a law over `{a, g: {c, e}}`, whose paths are all kept and `x` moves to a path.
- **Notes:** "When the validity of two groupings differs"; "The conditions on renaming"; "Optional inputs across merges, and their consequences" (source § 4.16 items 1 to 6); "Dimension scopes across merges" (source § 4.17).
- **Source:** source § 4.15, § 4.16, § 4.17; invariants I15 to I22.

#### 5.6 What merge is not

- **Defines:** none.
- **States:** merge is not composition, conditioning, or renaming.
- **Figures:** none.
- **Examples:** one line each.
- **Notes:** none.
- **Source:** source § 2.22.

### 6. The kinds, one by one

Each subsection has the same order: what it is; what it produces; its parts; its inputs; its outputs; what merge means for it; a figure; a small standalone example.
It states only what is specific to the kind, and points to §§ 1 to 5 for the general rules.

#### 6.1 Records and base values

- **Defines:** none new.
- **States:** a record is its own produced term and offers its children; a base value standing alone has no outputs; records merge side by side; arrays and opaque values do not merge.
- **Figures:** `record-parts` (a record with its outputs).
- **Examples:** a draw `{a, g: {c, e}}`; the record `{n: 3.0, G: post}`.
- **Notes:** none.
- **Source:** source § 5.2, § 5.3, § 5.4.

#### 6.2 Laws

- **Defines:** *conditioning* (the law of the rest of a law given values at some of its paths); *summary* (a mean, variance, standard deviation, or quantile).
- **States:** a law's paths are its event type's paths (§ 2.4), so for a law that can be sampled, selecting commutes with sampling; a marginal law at a list of paths keeps the paths; conditioning removes what it fixes; a summary that has one value per field keeps the law's paths and form, and binds where a draw of its type binds; a law whose draws are laws, functions, or batches is legal and offers no outputs; a family names its own law's draws, wrapping them when they are not base values.
- **Figures:** `law` (a law over `{mu, tau}` and its event type); `law-of-laws` (a random measure's draws wrapped under `G`).
- **Examples:** a law over `{a, g: {c, e}}`, its marginal at `g` and at `[g]`; conditioning a law over `{mu, tau}` on `mu`; the mean of a law keeps its paths; a law whose draws are laws, wrapped and not.
- **Notes:** "Conditioning at nested paths, and on records"; "Scoring a summary"; "A family that names its own law's draws".
- **Source:** source § 5.5 (all of it, without the "Today" blocks), § 2.11 (law-valued produced terms); I2, I3, I7, I8, I11.

#### 6.3 Kernels

- **Defines:** none new.
- **States:** a kernel has at least one input; a kernel whose inputs are all optional is still a kernel; binding the last required input gives a law; no name is both an input and an output; selecting keeps the inputs.
- **Figures:** `signature` (reused).
- **Examples:** `lik(y | mu)` and `lik_opt(y | mu, s)`; selecting a kernel whose draws are records.
- **Notes:** "Why a kernel and the function that returns its laws have different produced terms".
- **Source:** source § 5.6, § 2.9 (disjoint sides, attribute roles, conceptually).

#### 6.4 Functions

- **Defines:** none new.
- **States:** a function's outputs are its return value's; a function into records is the record of its output functions; a function that returns a law offers no outputs, and the code that wants a name wraps the law in its return value; a function that returns a function offers that function's outputs; functions merge as kernels whose laws are point masses.
- **Figures:** `function` (a function into `{mean, var}` and its output functions).
- **Examples:** the emulator from `theta` to `{mean, var}` at `mean`; a fit that returns a posterior, positionally and wrapped; `center & spread`.
- **Notes:** "A function without a declared return type" (paths checked at the call).
- **Source:** source § 5.7.

#### 6.5 Batches

- **Defines:** *element type* (the output spec of one element: for a base element, under the element's name); *equal levels*.
- **States:** a batch is indexed by its elements' paths; its outputs are its elements' outputs, as columns; a batch built from bare values names a base element `element`; two batches over equal levels merge element by element; names stay out of a batch's equality.
- **Figures:** `batch` (100 draws of a law over `{mu, tau}` and its columns).
- **Examples:** 100 draws of a law over `{a, g: {c, e}}`; 4 draws of a law over one array `x`; merging two batches of draws.
- **Notes:** "Why names stay out of a batch's equality" (conceptual reason only; the JAX detail goes to § 18).
- **Source:** source § 5.8, § 2.9 (element spec), § 6.21.

### 7. Invariants

#### 7.1 The invariants

- **Defines:** *equal* for values, laws, and functions (the meaning of `==` in the invariants).

## Ambiguities and contradictions found

Each becomes a question in Appendix A, and the body takes the reading given here.

1. **When the library stops building the interim whole form.** Source § 6.8 says every part of the library but the G2 families stops building it once G1b merges, while T19, § 5.5, and the probe's allowance (§ 6.14) let views at held fields build it until G3, which follows the workshop. The source itself marks T19 "Inconsistent". Reading: T19's (until G3).
2. **"Has parts" without parts.** The source calls every law and every batch a term "with parts", including a law over a single array and a batch of arrays, which have no paths below their root. The word "part" as a noun is never defined. The rewrite defines a part as a node below the root, and states the naming rule as "only a base value is named from outside", which is what the source's rule decides; it drops "spec with parts" and "has parts". Reading: unchanged behaviour; the question is only whether the maintainer accepts the wording.
3. **Membership on a function whose return type is unknown.** `f[s]` on such a function succeeds and checks `s` at the call (source § 5.7), while its type has no paths (§ 2.10). I18 says `key in t` exactly when `t[key]` succeeds, so `"anything" in f` would be true. Reading: none chosen; the body states both rules and the question asks which governs `in`.
4. **`without` on batches of functions and of kernels.** Source § 4.5 lists `without` on functions, kernels, batches of records, and batches of laws, but not on batches of functions or kernels, although a batch is indexed by its elements' paths. Reading: not defined for them.
5. **`in` on an opaque value.** I18 covers "every term other than an array", but an opaque value has no `[]` (§ 4.1). Whether `"a" in opaque` is `False` or raises is not stated. Reading: `False`, by I18.
6. **Whether a node below a held term is a field or a group.** A field is "named through records only" (§ 2.4), while a group has no such clause, so a record node inside a held law's draw may or may not be a group. This affects `without`'s rule that an emptied group goes. Reading: such a node is neither; `without` removes a held field whose every leaf path it covers, as § 4.5 says.
7. **The word for IV.2's "unmet".** The source has S0 write "unfed" into `design/`. This document retires "unfed" in favour of plain words such as "the inputs that remain". Reading: the body says "remaining inputs"; Appendix B keeps the source's instruction to S0, and the question asks whether `design/` should use the plain words too.
8. **Whether the type of a law and the type of a kernel merge.** Source § 2.16 lists "specs of laws, kernels, functions, and batches: two of one of these", which can be read as two specs of one class or as two specs of one merge category. Terms of laws and kernels form one category. Reading: a law's type and a kernel's type merge, as their terms do.

Not a question, and recorded in Appendix B instead: property 3 of source § 4.15 gives `a & b` and `b & a` equal input specs once a shared input carries the unification, while transition row T29 keeps the first operand's type until after the workshop, so until then their input specs can differ.
